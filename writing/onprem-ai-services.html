On-prem AI is a services business

Yunseop Im · Opinion · 2026 · ~8 min read

Every enterprise AI vendor now leads with the same pitch: "your data never leaves your building." On-premise LLM deployments are the hottest category in the market, and the sales decks all show the same architecture diagram — GPUs in a rack, a serving engine, an API, done.

I've operated one of these deployments in production. I'm going to say the thing the decks don't: what you're buying isn't software. It's an operational relationship — and it's priced like neither.

The demo hides the employment contract

A cloud API call has a clean boundary. You send a request, you get a response, the provider absorbs everything in between — uptime, model updates, capacity, failures at 3am. The contract is per-token and the responsibility is theirs.

On-prem inverts every part of that. When inference runs on the customer's GPUs, "the provider absorbs failures" becomes "someone absorbs failures" — and that someone is whoever signed the support contract. The questions that decide whether the deployment lives or dies aren't in the sales demo:

  • When the GPU thermal-throttles during a batch job, who gets paged?
  • When the model produces a confidently wrong answer, who owns the quality regression — and how would they even detect it?
  • When the customer wants a newer model, who runs the eval suite, signs off on the quality delta, and manages the rollback?
  • When traffic doubles, who provisions GPUs on a six-week lead time?

None of these are engineering questions. They're service-desk questions — and they exist for the entire life of the deployment, not the first sprint.

Why vendors underprice it

The incentive structure guarantees underpricing. An on-prem deal priced like a SaaS contract — license fee plus support hours — looks great on the vendor's P&L until the first incident. The honest pricing model is closer to a managed service: deployment + continuous operations + model lifecycle management + quality assurance, priced as an annuity, not a license.

Vendors resist this because it breaks the software narrative. "We sell you a box and leave" is a much easier sale than "we become part of your on-call rotation." But the deployments that succeed are the ones where both sides admitted what the contract actually is.

What buyers should ask before signing

If you're evaluating an on-prem AI deployment, the demo answers none of the questions that matter. Ask these instead:

  1. "Show me your incident history, anonymized." Not the uptime SLA — the actual failure modes you've hit in production and how they were resolved. A vendor who's operated this will have war stories. A vendor who's only demoed it will deflect.
  2. "What does the quality-regression process look like on a model update?" If the answer is "we'll retrain and redeploy," the vendor doesn't understand that the model is frozen and every config change is a release. You want golden-set evals, rollback criteria, and a named owner.
  3. "Who owns the pager after go-live?" Get it in the contract. "Vendor supports it" with no named rotation, escalation path, or response-time commitment is a wish, not a service.
  4. "What happens when our traffic doubles?" GPU capacity isn't elastic. You need a pre-agreed answer — queue, degrade, or provision — with lead times and cost ownership written down.
  5. "Show me a customer reference who ran this for a year." Not a launch reference — a renewal reference. The churn conversations are where the truth lives.

The reframe that makes it work

None of this means on-prem AI is a bad bet — for regulated data, IP-sensitive work, and air-gapped environments it's often the only option. The reframe is simpler: price and staff it as the service it is.

For vendors: build the operational muscle before the sales motion. The teams that win on-prem aren't the ones with the best model demos — they're the ones who can answer "who gets paged at 2am" without pausing.

For buyers: the cheapest on-prem proposal is usually the most expensive one. The proposal that includes named operational ownership, quality gates, and capacity planning is the one that will still be running in year two.

On-prem AI isn't a product you buy once. It's a service you employ forever. Price it, staff it, and contract it accordingly — or don't sign.
