GraphRAG for engineering data — why PLM documents break naive RAG

1. The retrieval problem nobody demos

Every RAG demo uses the same corpus: PDFs of prose. Wikipedia articles, product documentation, support tickets — text written for humans to read linearly. Chunk it, embed it, cosine-similarity it, done.

Engineering data is not that. In a PLM (product lifecycle management) corpus — the system of record for how physical products get designed, revised, and manufactured — the documents are versioned, relational, and scope-bound:

  • BOMs (bills of materials) are tables, not paragraphs — a parent assembly pointing at hundreds of child parts with quantities, effectivity dates, and revision pins.
  • Revisions mean the same part number refers to different physical objects across time. "Bracket, PN-4471" in rev A and rev C are different parts wearing the same name.
  • ECOs (engineering change orders) describe changes, not states — "ECO-2291 replaces fastener F-88 with F-91 on assemblies A-12 through A-19, effective March." The answer to "what changed?" lives in the relationship between documents, not inside any one of them.
  • Drawings and specs cross-reference each other by number — "see note 4," "per DWG-2210," "dimensions in mm unless noted." The referent of half the sentences is another document entirely.

The queries users actually ask are relational too:

  • "Which assemblies still use fastener F-88 after ECO-2291?"
  • "What changed on PN-4471 between rev B and rev C?"
  • "Show me every part affected by supplier S's recall notice."
  • "What's the current revision of the housing, and which ECO approved it?"

None of these are answerable by finding the most similar text chunk. They require traversing relationships. That's the gap GraphRAG exists to fill — and it's why "we tried RAG on our engineering docs and it hallucinated" is the most common outcome in this domain.

2. Why chunk-and-embed fails on engineering data

Naive RAG fails on engineering corpora for four specific, mechanical reasons — not because embeddings are bad, but because the retrieval unit is wrong.

2.1 The chunk boundary destroys the structure

Standard chunking splits documents at token counts — 512 tokens, 50% overlap, whatever. Applied to a BOM table, this produces chunks like:

... F-88  M6x20 SHCS  4  REV-C ... PN-4471 BRACKET ...
   A-12 ASSY TOP COVER ... 2 ... F-91 ...

A chunk containing "F-88" without the row's assembly context, quantity, or effectivity is not a smaller version of the truth — it's a different statement. The model receives fragments that look authoritative and aren't.

2.2 Embeddings can't represent "current" vs "historical"

Rev A and rev C of the same document are near-identical in embedding space — they share vocabulary, structure, and intent. Cosine similarity will happily retrieve both, and rank the obsolete one equally. There is no semantic signal for "this revision is superseded" because supersession is a graph fact, not a text feature.

2.3 Identifiers aren't semantics

"PN-4471" embeds as noise — a part number has no semantic neighborhood. Dense retrieval is built on semantic similarity, and engineering data runs on identifier joins. The question "which drawings reference PN-4471" is a lookup problem dressed as a search problem.

2.4 Multi-hop questions have no single source chunk

"Which assemblies use F-88 after ECO-2291" requires: find the ECO → extract the affected assembly range → check each assembly's current BOM → filter by effectivity. No chunk contains that answer. The answer is a computation over relationships that retrieval must assemble first.

The pattern: naive RAG answers "what does this document say." Engineering questions ask "what is true now, across documents, given how they relate." Those are different information problems, and only the second one needs a graph.

3. What a knowledge graph actually buys you

A knowledge graph represents the corpus as entities and relationships instead of text spans:

Part("PN-4471") ──has_revision──▶ Revision("C")
Revision("C") ──supersedes──▶ Revision("B")
Assembly("A-12") ──contains──▶ Part("F-88")
ECO("ECO-2291") ──affects──▶ Assembly("A-12".."A-19")
ECO("ECO-2291") ──replaces──▶ Part("F-88") ──with──▶ Part("F-91")
Drawing("DWG-2210") ──depicts──▶ Part("PN-4471")

Now the failing queries become traversals:

  • "What changed on PN-4471 between rev B and C?" → walk the supersedes edge, diff the two revision nodes' attributes.
  • "Which assemblies still use F-88 after ECO-2291?" → expand ECO-2291's affects range, traverse each assembly's contains edges, filter by the ECO's effective date and current revision.
  • "What does DWG-2210 specify?" → follow depicts to the part, then has_revision to the current revision — not whichever revision the embedding happened to rank first.

This is what "GraphRAG" means in practice: graph traversal produces candidate context that flat retrieval can't reach, then the LLM answers over that assembled context with citations. The graph doesn't replace the LLM — it fixes what the LLM gets to see.

4. Architecture: from documents to cited answers

4.1 Ingestion: structure first, embeddings second

The critical difference from prose RAG: parse for structure before you embed anything.

  • Tables stay tables. A BOM row becomes a structured record — {parent, child, qty, effectivity, revision} — not a text span. Retrieval over rows is a filter+join, not a similarity search.
  • Identifiers become entities. Part numbers, drawing numbers, ECO IDs are extracted as entity mentions and resolved to canonical nodes. This is where the graph gets its backbone.
  • Cross-references become edges. "See DWG-2210," "per note 4," "supersedes rev B" are parsed into typed relationships at ingest time.
  • Scope metadata is mandatory. Every chunk, entity, and edge carries its tenant, document set, and effective date. Without this, nothing downstream can answer "current" correctly.

4.2 Retrieval: three signals, one ranking

Production retrieval here is hybrid by necessity:

  1. BM25 (sparse) — catches exact identifiers: part numbers, drawing numbers, ECO IDs. Dense retrieval is famously bad at these; BM25 is famously good.
  2. Dense (embeddings) — catches semantic questions: "what's the finish spec for exterior parts" matches chunks that never share vocabulary with the query.
  3. Graph expansion — given entities resolved from the query (and from the top sparse/dense hits), traverse 1–2 hops to pull in connected context the text match never surfaced: the ECO that affected the assembly, the revision that superseded the part, the drawing that depicts it.

The fusion step reranks the union. In our reference implementation this is BM25 + graph-expansion signals fused into a single ranking — deliberately simple, because the interesting failure modes are upstream in extraction, not in the ranker.

4.3 Generation: constrained, cited, scoped

The final layer reuses the serving patterns from the on-prem field guide: guided decoding constrains output to a schema — {answer, citations[], confidence} — where each citation is a pointer into the retrieved context, not a model-generated reference. The model structurally cannot cite a document that wasn't retrieved. Below a confidence threshold, the system escalates rather than guesses — in engineering domains, a confident wrong revision reference is worse than no answer.

5. Multi-tenancy and the graph

Engineering data is almost always multi-tenant: each customer has their own product lines, their own part numbering, their own revision histories. The graph inherits the isolation problem of the corpus — a traversal that crosses tenant boundaries is a data leak, and a query that can't express tenant scope returns another customer's parts.

The working pattern: tenant scope is a first-class filter applied at every traversal step, enforced server-side in the query layer — never trusted from the client. Concretely:

  • Every node and edge carries tenant_id; graph queries include it as a mandatory predicate, not an optional filter.
  • Entity resolution is tenant-scoped — "PN-4471" in tenant A and tenant B are different parts; a shared entity namespace silently merges them.
  • For smaller deployments, per-tenant graphs or scoped subgraphs are viable; at scale, a shared graph with mandatory scope predicates is simpler to operate than N isolated graphs — the same trade-off as shared vector stores with metadata filters.

This is the same isolation discipline as multi-tenant RAG generally — the graph doesn't change the boundary problem, it just makes the boundary structural rather than a post-filter.

6. Honest limits

GraphRAG for engineering data is not free, and the costs are specific:

  • Entity extraction is the bottleneck. The graph is only as good as the parser that built it. Engineering documents are inconsistent — same part numbered three ways across suppliers, tables in non-standard layouts, scans without text layers. Expect the ingestion pipeline to be the majority of the engineering effort, and expect it to need per-corpus tuning.
  • Graph construction is a batch job, not a stream. Revisions and ECOs arrive continuously; the graph needs incremental update semantics — a new ECO must update affected edges without rebuilding the corpus.
  • Not every question needs a graph. "Summarize this spec" is a chunk retrieval problem. Reserve graph expansion for queries that resolve to entities and relationships — unconditional graph traversal adds latency and noise to questions that didn't need it.
  • Stale graphs lie confidently. If the graph lags the source documents, it will produce well-cited answers about a world that no longer exists. Freshness monitoring is a correctness requirement, not an ops nicety.

The honest summary: GraphRAG earns its complexity when the corpus is relational and the questions are multi-hop. For prose corpora with topical questions, it's overhead. Engineering data is the former — which is why this is one of the few domains where the graph approach isn't a solution looking for a problem.

7. Reference implementation

The architecture above is implemented in graphrag-lab — a zero-dependency Python/SQLite reference: section-aware chunking, entity and relation extraction, graph expansion fused with BM25 scoring, and a CLI for indexing and querying corpora. It's deliberately minimal — the point is to make the mechanism legible, not to ship a platform.
